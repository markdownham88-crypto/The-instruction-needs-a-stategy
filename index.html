The Instruction Needs a Strategy

Every secured loan is decided by a team that exists for a few weeks and appears on no organisation chart: the lender's functions, the valuer, the borrower, the broker and introducer, and two firms of solicitors. This paper is about how that team forms, how it works under pressure, and why the valuation it produces is so often lent against as if it were a guarantee.

The question. Why do valuation reports that were broadly right at the valuation date still sit behind losses that surprise lenders at enforcement — and what can the lender, the valuer and everyone else round the table do differently on the next instruction?

The argument in four steps. (1) A valuation instruction calls a temporary organisation into being, and its quality depends on how it is configured. (2) When a loan's future is contested, that organisation needs islands of certainty — shared, deliberately built understandings — and reliability imported from the standing organisations behind it. (3) Different loans need different conversations, which the strategy palette names: Classical, Adaptive, Visionary, Shaping and Renewal. (4) Beneath every secured loan lies a strategic game, in which the rewards for completing the transaction, for confirming the figure the facility requires and for treating the valuer's professional indemnity cover as a surrogate for credit judgement progressively erode the very information the valuation is commissioned to supply.

I.

The instruction is an organisation

A valuation instruction does not describe an organisation. It calls one into being.

Every lender has an instruction template. It names the property, the borrower, the purpose, the basis of value, the special assumptions, the reliance parties, the fee and the deadline. Procurement treats it as a ticket moving through states. When lending goes wrong, the failure is pinned on a node: the valuation was "wrong", the valuer "optimistic".

Anyone who has sat on either side of a difficult instruction knows that this is not what happens. What happens is a set of conversations running at once across boundaries that were never designed to be permeable. The relationship manager rings the valuer to "give some colour". The valuer rings back because the floor areas in the particulars do not match the lease plan. The broker writes to say the comparable the valuer "is obviously going to use" is not comparable. The solicitor finds a right of way nobody mentioned. Credit asks whether the 180-day assumption is realistic. For a few days or weeks these people are organised — they share a task, a deadline, an interdependence and an exposure — and then they disperse.

Lundin and Söderholm's four features of a temporary organisation are all present: time (a bounded life ending in a report and a decision), task (a complex, non-routine judgement of value and risk), team (a cross-firm assembly with no common line manager) and transition (a loan proposition becoming a commitment). If that is right, "was the valuation correct?" is an impoverished question. The richer one is: how well did the temporary organisation that formed around this instruction coordinate — and was it playing the right game?

Tactic

Before the next difficult instruction, write down who is actually in the configuration — not who is named on the template. Include the person nobody invites: whoever will read this file if the loan fails.

In depth · Why the configuration is invisible

The configuration is invisible because each party's own organisation draws its boundary at the edge of its contract. The lender sees a supplier; the valuer sees a client; the broker sees a completion; the solicitors see a retainer. Nobody's chart shows the twelve-handed organisation that actually forms. That invisibility is also why the valuation report gets treated as a product rather than an outcome of organising — and why, when the loan fails, the instinct is to sue the product's maker rather than ask how the organisation coordinated.

The guarantee reading of a valuation is the purest expression of that instinct. If the report is a product, its defects are the maker's liability and its insurance is the lender's recourse. If the report is the output of a temporary organisation the lender itself convened, framed and fed, then most of what went wrong was organised by the lender too. The law has long recognised this: in Platform Home Loans the House of Lords allowed a lender's own imprudent lending to reduce its damages against the valuer.

The cast: everyone round the instruction

The lender–valuer relationship is never a duet. It is a twelve-handed game in which half the players are not named on the instruction.

Talk about "the lender" and "the valuer" hides most of what happens. The lender is at least four people with different interests: origination, which wants the deal; credit, which wants a loss it can live with; the panel manager, who wants a clean, independent, quick panel; and workout, which wants maximum recovery if things go wrong. The valuer faces all four, often at different moments in the same loan.

Round them sit the parties whose information and incentives shape everything. The borrower knows more about the asset than anyone and has every reason to present it well. The broker packages the deal and is paid on completion; in commercial lending much broking sits outside FCA regulation, governed by contract and trade codes such as the NACFB's. The introducer may see the deal being assembled before anyone else, and is paid for the introduction. Two sets of solicitors hold the legal truth of title, leases and contracts — the lender's solicitor, in residential lending, under duties set by the UK Finance Mortgage Lenders' Handbook, including reporting where the seller has owned the property for under six months. Behind them all stand the receiver, the configuration's latent last member, and the regulator and RICS, who write the rules of the game.

Two courtroom lessons from the edges of the table

Commission. Brokers are often paid by both sides. In Wood v Commercial First (2021) the Court of Appeal held that a borrower could obtain relief, including rescission, where a lender paid a broker a commission that was not properly disclosed, without having to prove a fiduciary relationship. In Johnson v FirstRand (August 2025) the Supreme Court held that motor dealers acting as credit brokers did not owe customers a fiduciary duty, while confirming that where a fiduciary duty of loyalty does apply, the principal must be told all material facts. For the configuration, the lesson is practical: what the broker is paid, and by whom, is a fact about how hard the deal is being pushed — and the valuer almost never knows it.

Identity and trust. In the linked P&P Property and Dreamvar appeals (2018), solicitors were held liable for breach of trust in identity frauds even where they had not been negligent. Solicitors hold money and title on trust; the configuration's legal backbone is stronger than its valuation backbone, and the two are rarely connected.

Tactic

On every non-classical instruction, name all twelve roles — even if three are "none". The act of naming shows which information will never reach the valuer.

The lender opened out: seven functions, six cultures

"The lender" is a fiction of the facility agreement. Inside it are relationship, origination, credit, portfolio management, business support, risk and recovery — functions that merge, split, migrate and change places with the cycle.

The cast in the previous section gave the lender four faces: origination, credit, the panel manager and workout. That was a simplification, and for the valuer a dangerous one. A valuer instructed on a secured loan is not dealing with an institution but with whichever of the lender's functions happens to hold the file — and the identity of that function changes as the loan moves from lead to sanction, from completion to monitoring, from the watchlist into distress and, finally, into the hands of a receiver. Each function reads the same report for a different purpose. Each wants something different from the valuer. And the way the functions are arranged — which are merged in one person, which are separated by a chain of command, which hold power at which stage of the cycle — differs sharply from one lending culture to another.

This section opens the lender out. It names seven functions, follows them through six UK lending cultures, and shows what their arrangement does to the relationship with the valuer and with everyone else round the table. The argument is simple: the valuer's real client is a configuration of lender functions that is itself temporary, fluid and culture-specific. Most failures of the lender–valuer relationship are failures to know which configuration is on the other end of the phone.

Seven functions

Relationship. The relationship function owns the customer over time. In a clearing bank the relationship manager may hold a whole connection — the investment loan, the trading company's overdraft, the director's guarantee, the next deal — and is judged on its total profitability. Relationship is the lender's memory of the borrower, and its best contribution to a valuation is context only it holds: the borrower's track record, what happened last time, the real exit. Its characteristic distortion is protective: a down-valuation threatens more than one facility, so relationship reads revaluations as threats to the customer rather than as information about the security, and it holds stressed customers too long before passing them on.

Origination. Origination finds, prices and wins the loan. It lives with brokers and introducers, writes the term sheet, promises the speed and is paid, formally or informally, on volume and margin. In many lenders origination and relationship are the same person; in others origination is a separate sales or intermediary team. It is the main channel for frame pressure on the valuer — the "colour call", the hint about the required figure, the broker who knows which valuer "understands the deal" — and at its best the earliest warning that a deal has been mis-coloured.

Credit. Credit underwrites and sanctions: a credit committee with delegated authorities in a bank, an investment committee in a fund, an underwriter in a bridging lender. It is the ideal reader of the report as a risk-management instrument — interested in the risks to the security, the depth of the market, the forced-sale value and what the figure depends on. It is also where the slide from instrument to guarantee begins: under volume pressure, credit stops reading the report and starts relying on it.

Portfolio management. After completion, someone must watch the book: covenant tests on loan-to-value and interest cover, periodic revaluations, information undertakings, concentration by sector and region, maturities and refinancing risk. In a syndicate the facility agent does much of this mechanically. Portfolio management wants revaluations that are comparable over time and across the book; its characteristic distortion is to turn revaluation into a covenant ritual, in which the figure that would trigger a breach is negotiated rather than examined and the valuer is asked to "look again".

Business support. Between performing and defaulted lies a function with many names — business support, restructuring, special situations, intensive care. It takes loans showing stress: the watchlist, early-warning triggers, restructuring, turnaround, sometimes debt-for-equity or loan-to-own. Business support is often the first function to commission a valuation for a decision rather than for compliance, and it wants value on several bases: as is, with vacant possession, with and without the business, on a forced-sale timetable. It is also where the lender's functions collide most violently, as the next part of this section shows.

Risk. The second line of defence writes credit policy and risk appetite, owns valuation policy and panel standards, runs models and stress tests, and reports to the regulator. Under the PRA's Basel 3.1 rules, from 1 January 2027 the value of property used for capital purposes must meet the regulator's valuation requirements, which pulls risk closer to the instruction than it has been. Risk wants independence, consistency and auditability. In large banks it is often too remote — writing the policy and never seeing the instruction; in many non-bank lenders it is too thin to matter. The PRA's 2024 thematic letter to non-systemic deposit-takers noted firms where quality assurance was performed by only one of the two lines of defence, and urged some to strengthen portfolio management controls.

Recovery. Recovery enforces: it appoints receivers or administrators, decides whether to sell or hold, pursues guarantors, weighs debt sale and — if the loss is bad enough — reviews whether the valuer was negligent. Recovery reads the original valuation twice: once as a recovery map, and once as a claim file. The second reading is the guarantee reading made explicit, and its shadow falls backwards over every instruction the valuer accepts.

Fluid, not fixed

These are functions, not departments. They can be arranged in many ways, and the arrangement moves in four directions at once.

They merge. In a debt fund one deal team sources the loan, underwrites it to the investment committee, manages it, restructures it and, if need be, enforces. In a challenger bank a relationship director finds, manages and watches the loan until it defaults. Merger preserves memory — the person who framed the instruction is still there when it goes wrong — and it concentrates self-review: the team that wrote the loan judges its own distress.

They split. In a clearing bank the seven functions may sit in five or six different reporting lines. Separation is the classical answer to capture and the regulator's preferred shape. Its cost is the hand-off: each time the file moves between functions, what the first team knew about how the instruction was framed — the hesitation in the valuer's voice, the condition that was argued away, the fact the broker mentioned once — travels only if someone has written it down.

They migrate. Over the life of a single loan, ownership of the file moves: from origination at the lead, to credit at sanction, back to relationship at completion, to portfolio management, to whoever holds the watchlist, to business support in distress and to recovery at enforcement. The valuer who was instructed by one function is read, years later, by another that has never heard of the first.

They change places with the cycle. In the boom, origination and relationship are the lender's centre of gravity; credit is under pressure to keep up; business support and recovery are small and quiet. In the bust the weight reverses: risk tightens, business support swells, recovery becomes the most powerful function in the building and the most attentive reader of old valuations. The palette colours of Section IV describe the loan; the cycle describes which function's colour dominates the lender's reading of it.

Six lending cultures

The UK clearing bank is the classic separation: relationship banking in the front line, credit sanction and risk as independent lines, portfolio monitoring in credit or risk, a large business-support unit and a recoveries function that appoints receivers. Valuations are usually instructed through a central valuation or panel function, sometimes through an outsourced panel manager, and relationship managers are told not to talk to valuers about figures. The danger here is less capture than fragmentation — seven functions, each reading the same report for a different purpose, none owning the whole story.

The challenger or specialist bank is lean and relationship-led. A relationship director finds the deal, manages the customer and watches the loan; credit and risk often sit together; a small collections and recoveries team takes defaults and moves quickly to receivership. Fewer hand-offs keep memory; they also keep optimism, because the relationship director marks their own homework from lead to watchlist.

The building society is mutual, policy-led and, in commercial lending, usually small. Deals arrive through intermediaries, are underwritten centrally and are governed by a board risk committee with a long memory of the 1990s. Risk dominates, and the configuration's typical failure is the reverse of the bank's: slow decisions, over-caution and good loans lost to faster lenders.

The debt fund merges almost everything into one deal team, with an investment committee as credit and a risk function that is often thin; a back-leverage lender may act as a de facto risk function by imposing its own valuation requirements. Its share of the market is no longer marginal. Bayes Business School reported new UK commercial real estate lending of £52.7bn in 2025, with debt funds lifting their share of new lending from 12% to 28%, and UK banks' share falling from 40% to 36%. Bayes also found alternative lenders' default rates above 20% in the first half of 2025. In the debt-fund culture, loan-to-own is a legitimate strategy, which changes what a valuation in distress is for: not only how much the loan will recover, but whether the lender wants the building.

The bridging lender is valuation-led lending at speed. Brokers bring the deal, the valuation is the underwriting in all but name, completion is measured in days and a missed redemption goes straight to LPA receivers. The guarantee reading is built into the business model: the loan is sized to the figure. Speed and fee pressure are greatest exactly where the report carries most weight.

The syndicate or club deal splits the functions across institutions. An arranger originates; each lender's credit sanctions its share on the same report; a facility agent administers covenants and orders revaluations mechanically; in distress a majority of lenders, sometimes through a co-ordinating committee, decides. Nobody owns the whole configuration, and in distress lenders' interests diverge — those who bought the debt at a discount and those holding at par read the same valuation as a negotiating instrument against each other. The London Approach, discussed in Section X, is the memory of how this used to be done collectively.

Business support: where the functions collide

Business support is where relationship, credit, portfolio management, risk and recovery meet over a single stressed loan, and where the configuration's incentives are least visible to the borrower. The most closely examined UK case is RBS's Global Restructuring Group. After Lawrence Tomlinson's 2013 report, the FCA appointed Promontory, with Mazars, as a skilled person to review RBS's treatment of SME customers transferred to GRG between 2008 and 2013. The review found no evidence that RBS artificially distressed and transferred otherwise viable SME businesses to profit from their restructuring or insolvency. It did find that many aspects of GRG's culture, governance and practices were deficient, and that in some areas the inappropriate treatment of customers was widespread and systematic. The FCA also noted that commercial lending is largely unregulated in the UK, and in July 2018 announced that it would not take enforcement action.

For this paper the lesson is structural rather than moral. When business support is a separate unit with its own targets, it can come to treat valuations as instruments of its own strategy: a low figure that justifies a covenant breach, a fee, a restructuring or a sale to an associated buyer. When business support barely exists, as in many lean lenders, stressed loans stay with the relationship function until they default, and the first honest valuation arrives too late to change anything. The valuer instructed by business support should ask the question this paper asks of every instruction: which function is framing this, and what does it want the figure to do?

What the fluidity does to the valuer — and to everyone else

For the valuer, the practical consequence is that the terms of engagement should name the function that frames the instruction, the purpose of the valuation and — increasingly — the functions that may later rely on it. A report written for sanction will be read for monitoring, for business support and for recovery; a report written for business support will be read by a receiver and, perhaps, by a court. The valuer cannot control those readings but can write for them: stating what the figure depends on, what bases were and were not considered, and what the valuer was and was not told.

For the borrower, the lender's fluidity is experienced as a change of personality. The relationship manager who welcomed the deal is replaced by a credit officer, then by an asset manager, then by a business-support director, then by a receiver. The borrower's solicitor and accountant need to know which function they are now dealing with, because each wants different things from a revaluation.

For brokers and introducers, the cultures are a market: they choose lenders partly by how their functions are arranged — a lender where origination and credit sit close, and where the valuer is reached easily, will complete faster. That choice selects for exactly the configurations in which the valuer is under most pressure.

For the solicitors, each function needs a different product: the report on title for credit, consents and waivers for portfolio management, security reviews for business support, enforcement-ready title for recovery. The lender's solicitor is often the only adviser who sees the file at every stage, and therefore the natural keeper of the instruction's memory across hand-offs.

For the receiver, the configuration that made the loan is the first thing to reconstruct. Who framed the instruction? Who held the file when the warning signs appeared? Was the valuation read as an instrument or relied on as a guarantee? The answers decide not only whether there is a claim but how the asset should be sold.

Tactic

On every instruction, write two lines into the file: which lender function framed the valuation, and which functions are expected to rely on it later. Re-read them at every hand-off.

II.

Islands of certainty

What a festival that lost its site in six weeks can teach a credit committee.

In June 2018, six weeks before its annual event, The Borderland — a participatory festival of around 2,500 co-creators run on Burning Man principles and Laloux's self-management — learned that Danish coastal-protection law put its long-used site off limits. It did not cancel. It scouted new sites, won new permits and re-planned all infrastructure, absorbing a ban on loud music and a national fire ban as it went. Verena Stingl and John McClellan analysed 751 pages of the community's real-time communication and found that coordination was neither simple integration nor simple fragmentation. Members produced islands of certainty: short-lived moments of shared understanding that gave orientation to, and licensed, the fragmented work around them.

Three practices built the islands. Framing re-created a shared sense of problem, purpose and boundaries, often by grounding the new situation in what the crisis had not changed. Co-creating issued open, specific invitations for help and worked on shared artefacts. Connecting relayed information between bubbles of activity — the work of people who called themselves "spiders in the net". And the pattern that matters most here: integration rises when the project's future is contested; fragmentation is high when the future is shared.

In depth · Islands that are relied on, and islands that are claimed on

An island of certainty is useful because it is provisional: everyone knows it holds only for now. A valuation report treated as a guarantee is an island asked to be permanent — to hold years later, in a different market, against a different question. That is the deepest category error of the guarantee reading.

The law's margin of error is the courts' recognition that the island was always provisional: competent valuers can differ by 5% on a standard house and 15% or more on unusual property. When a lender relies on the report as a fixed point, it relies on something the law itself describes as a range.

III.

Reliability without permanence

A high-reliability organisation is reliable because it remembers. The instruction must be reliable without a memory of its own.

The high-reliability literature grew from flight decks, air-traffic control and nuclear plants — places where error is likely and catastrophic, yet failure is rare. Weick and Sutcliffe distilled the practice into five principles: preoccupation with failure, reluctance to simplify, sensitivity to operations, commitment to resilience and deference to expertise. Classical HROs are permanent. Bigley and Roberts showed that the Incident Command System achieves the same reliability in temporary configurations by switching roles and migrating authority to whoever knows. Weick's account of Mann Gulch shows the opposite face: a temporary team whose sensemaking collapsed when its role system dissolved.

The lender–valuer configuration is a textbook case of teaming: people who may never have spoken coordinate through scaffolds — the instruction, the Red Book terms of engagement, panel accreditation, PI cover — and through swift trust extended on the strength of RICS registration. It has no memory of its own, so its reliability is imported from the standing organisations behind it. Score an instruction you know.

In depth · Insurance is not imported reliability

A high-reliability organisation imports reliability through habits, training and culture. PI insurance imports something else: a promise to pay, after the event, within limits. It is an import of recourse, not of reliability. Lenders who choose panel valuers chiefly by their cover levels are buying the wrong import — and RICS's own minimum limits of indemnity, scaled to a firm's turnover, are small beside the losses a single large loan can generate.

Worse, the insurance import can crowd out the reliability import. A lender confident of recourse has less reason to invest in framing calls, feedback and panel conversation — the things that would have made the claim unnecessary.

IV.

Your instruction needs a strategy

The strategy palette, read through the loan file.

Martin Reeves, Knut Haanaes and Janmejaya Sinha of BCG argue that the problem with strategy is not a shortage of tools but the lack of a way to choose among them. Their answer, the strategy palette, sorts environments along three dimensions: predictability (can you forecast it?), malleability (can you, alone or with others, shape it?) and harshness (can you survive it?). Five environments follow, each with its own imperative:

  • Classical — predictable, not malleable. Be big: analyse, plan, execute.
  • Adaptive — unpredictable, not malleable. Be fast: vary, select, scale, iterate.
  • Visionary — predictable to the visionary, malleable alone. Be first: envisage, build, persist.
  • Shaping — unpredictable, malleable with others. Be the orchestrator: engage, orchestrate, evolve the platform.
  • Renewal — harsh. Be viable: react early, economise, then pivot to another colour.

In their research, firms that matched approach to environment earned 4–8% more total shareholder return than those that did not — and around half mismatched in some way. They urge leaders to "de-average" a business, run several colours at once, and expect each business to travel round the palette over its life.

Every one of these environments has an instruction to match. A prime, long-let investment with deep evidence is classical. A sub-market moving under the valuer's feet is adaptive. A new asset class — the first data centre in a town, a build-to-rent block where there is no rental evidence — is visionary. A syndicated or securitised loan, a panel, a market standard, is shaping. A defaulted loan in receivership is renewal. Diagnose an instruction below.

Mismatch: the loan-book's characteristic error

If about half of companies mismatch their approach to their environment, the instruction desk is unlikely to do better. Four mismatches recur. A classical process applied to a visionary asset: a development or new-sector loan valued by template, with a residual appraisal standing in for a market that does not yet exist. A classical posture in an adaptive market: the autumn of 2022, when credit repriced within days and the instruction carried on as if the comparable set were still alive. A visionary posture in a renewal environment: "extend and pretend", which is a vision of recovery adopted because the alternative is a loss. And a classical response to renewal: a fire sale run like a routine disposal, where the receiver should have pivoted the asset into another colour first.

The palette also predicts a life-cycle. Reeves and colleagues observe that businesses tend to be born visionary or shaping and migrate counter-clockwise through adaptive to classical before being disrupted. A loan does the same. It is visionary at origination — a story about a future. It becomes adaptive as the market moves, classical once let and stable, and if it fails, enters renewal. The configuration around it must change colour as it travels. That is the integration paradox of Section VI, seen from the strategist's side.

The game beneath the loan

Strategy, to a manager, is a plan. To a game theorist it is a contingent plan of action. The instruction needs both.

Management scholars and game theorists use the word strategy differently and have rarely influenced each other, as Ram Shivakumar points out. Adam Brandenburger and Barry Nalebuff built the most useful bridge. In The Right Game and Co-opetition they argued that business success depends on playing the right game, and that a game can be changed through five elements — PARTS: Players, Added values, Rules, Tactics and Scope. A player's added value is what the game loses if that player is removed. And the value net places every player around the firm: customers, suppliers, competitors and complementors.

Shaping: the instruction as ecosystem micro-community

The shaping colour concerns ecosystems rather than single firms, and relies on collaboration as much as competition. Haier's RenDanHeYi model is the clearest working example. In 2012 Haier offered some 12,000 managers the choice of leaving or joining one of around 4,000 micro-enterprises. When micro-enterprises grew too inward-looking and user needs too complex, it built ecosystem micro-communities: several micro-enterprises bound by contract in a temporary alliance around one user scenario. Any of them can start one by publishing what it needs and how it will reward providers. Contracts share the value created, so a weak partner endangers everyone's income and helping is rational.

That is a valuation configuration that knows its own name. The UK valuation panel performs the same matching function — scenario to capability — but is run to route tickets, not to assemble communities. Its value-sharing is perverse: the valuer's fee is fixed, its exposure is all downside, and nobody's reward is tied to the quality of the coordination. Sharing loan performance would destroy independence. Sharing information — feedback, enforcement outcomes, the honest account of what happened — would not.

Change the game, not only the play. Add a player (a second valuer on a visionary asset), raise an added value (pay for the paragraph on risks to realisation), change a rule (feedback from recoveries), or widen the scope (judge a panel by its loss record, not its turnaround time).

In PARTS terms, the PI insurer is a player nobody names on the instruction. The file, as the receiver reads it

The receiver meets Mill Lane last and sees it whole. The building is on a secondary high street: a ground-floor shop let to a local operator on a lease with a tenant's break, two floors of small offices let on short terms, and a planning consent for twelve flats on the upper floors and in a rear extension. The borrower bought it fourteen months before the refinance and wanted to release equity to fund the residential works. A broker packaged the deal at £6.4m — seventy per cent of a "value" of £9.2m that appears in the pack as the borrower's appraisal of the completed scheme, discounted a little for works not yet done.

The valuer, allocated by the panel manager for its local office and residential evidence, found three things the pack did not mention. The shop was trading thinly and its owner was talking about using the break. The stair core could not serve the residential scheme without rework the cost estimate did not include. And, once the lender's solicitor reported on title, the rear yard — the only route for the scheme's bin store and cycle parking — turned out to be crossed by a neighbour's right of way. The valuer reported market value at £7.55m, about eighteen per cent below the figure the facility needed, and qualified the value of the completed scheme because of the access and the stair core.

What happened next depends on which version of the file the receiver is reading. In the usual version, the stair-core finding sat in paragraph 14 of a draft; the relationship manager rang the valuer for "colour" and the broker emailed a comparable; the title report went to the lender only; the figure arrived cold and origination asked for it to be "looked at again"; credit anchored somewhere between the report and the pack; and a £4.9m staged facility completed with covenants that said nothing about the risks the valuer had found. Eighteen months later the shop tenant broke, build costs rose and the works stalled. The covenants were waived, twice. When the borrower defaulted, workout instructed a new valuer without the original file, the right of way surfaced as a "new" problem, and the receivers sold the building as it stood at the bottom of the market.

In the configured version the same facts arrive, but they move sideways earlier. The colour is named at the lead. The terms are framed in a call, not just recorded. The valuer asks precise questions about arrears, break intentions and the build contract. The stair core reaches credit the day it is found. The broker has no channel to the valuer; origination's facts go in writing through the panel. The title report reaches the valuer before signature. Credit gatekeeps the low figure and then acts as secretary for Plan B, holding the boundary that the valuation is not being reopened. The principal risks are written into the report and into the covenants. At the drift the original valuer is asked for a desktop update; at the appointment the receiver reads the original report first, resolves the right of way and sells to a wider field. The appraisal game

When the valuer is paid to inform and rewarded for confirming, information goes missing.

The clearest empirical picture of the lender–valuer game comes from the United States. Researchers at the Philadelphia Fed found that home-purchase appraisals rarely come in below the contract price: some 30% of appraisals in their sample were exactly at the price, with fewer than 10% below it. They argue that institutional features of mortgage lending cause much of the information in appraisals to be lost. Inflated appraisals during the subprime boom led New York's Attorney General, the government-sponsored enterprises and their regulator to adopt the Home Valuation Code of Conduct in 2009, which separated loan production staff from appraiser selection.

The credit-rating agencies played a cousin of this game. Bolton, Freixas and Shapiro modelled two conflicts: raters understating risk to win business, and issuers shopping for the best rating. They found that competition can reduce efficiency by enabling shopping, and that ratings are more likely to be inflated in booms, when trusting investors are many and reputational risk feels low. Replace "rating" with "valuation" and "issuer" with "originator" and the model describes the boom-origination culture of every UK property crash.

Game theory adds a third insight. When a sender and receiver want different outcomes, cheap talk becomes coarse: the message collapses into a few categories. A valuation that clusters at "confirms the required value" or "does not" has been coarsened in exactly this way. Clustering is not proof of bias. It is evidence that the game is eating the information.

The integration paradox, measured

Stingl and McClellan found that integration rose when the future was contested and fell when it was shared. Translate that into the loan book: every instruction sits somewhere on a line from "nobody doubts this loan's future" to "everything about it is contested". Every instruction also gets some amount of lender–valuer contact — calls, framing, challenge, escalation. A process design decides how contact is distributed. The figure plots twenty composite instructions from across the market and lets you change the design.

How the two axes are built. Contest — across the figure — is a judgement of how much about the loan's future is genuinely in doubt. It combines six things: the depth and currency of comparable evidence; the security of the income; how far the value depends on something not yet true, such as a scheme, a licence or a turnaround; title and legal complexity; the borrower's experience and the clarity of the exit; and the direction and liquidity of the market. A prime logistics shed let for fifteen years scores close to zero; a data-centre conversion with no evidence and uncertain power supply scores close to a hundred. Contact — up the figure — is the amount of lender–valuer interaction the instruction receives: a framing call at instruction, contact with credit while the work is in progress, title findings passed across, and calls or emails from origination and brokers. The last of these is contact too, but of a particular kind: it carries pressure more often than information.

The game, drawn as a tree

The appraisal game is sequential. Origination decides whether to press; the valuer then decides whether to confirm or report independently. Solving it backwards — asking first what the valuer will do in each case — shows when pressure pays. The payoffs below are illustrative, but the structure follows the models above: inflation pays most in booms, when completion is worth most and reputation feels cheapest, and least where the game is repeated and recovery outcomes are fed back.

The four levers. C is the value of completion to origination: the fee, the margin, the volume target, the broker relationship. Brokers, borrowers and introducers push it up; a falling market pushes it down. E is the expected loss origination bears if the loan is overvalued: how far credit, risk and the lender's own incentive structure make origination carry the consequences of a bad figure. Credit's challenge, the solicitor's title findings and a downturn push it up. K is the valuer's stake in the panel: how much future work depends on pleasing this originator. Rotation, panel entry and separation of origination from instruction push it down. R is the valuer's reputation and liability cost of confirming a figure the evidence does not support: claims, PI excesses, RICS regulation and — above all — recovery outcomes fed back to the valuer push it up.

The payoffs. If origination does not press and the valuer reports independently, origination gets half the completion value (the deal is restructured) and the valuer its baseline of 2, plus a small credit if feedback recognises good work. If origination does not press and the valuer confirms anyway, origination gets the full completion value less the expected loss, and the valuer pays the reputational cost. Pressing costs origination half a unit of effort and relationship. If origination presses and the valuer confirms, the valuer collects its panel stake K and pays R; if it presses and the valuer holds, the valuer loses K. Recovery feedback adds 1.5 to the cost of confirming. The table beneath the tree shows each payoff as the levers move.

What the model leaves out. It is a two-move game with complete information. With uncertainty the clean outcomes blur into mixed strategies — sometimes pressing, sometimes confirming — and that blur is what the clustering of valuations at the required figure looks like from outside. The model also treats the lender as one player. Section III shows why that is false: credit raises E, the panel manager sets K, recovery raises R, and origination chooses whether to press. The game is played inside the lender as much as between lender and valuer.

Instrument or guarantee? The valuation report and the insurance illusion

A valuation is built to inform a lending decision. Too often it is lent against as though it insured the loan.

Every valuation report can be read two ways. Read as a risk-management instrument, it is information: a professional opinion of value and of the risks to the security, given within a margin of error, for a defined purpose, to help a lender decide whether and how much to lend. The lender keeps the credit risk and the market risk; the valuer is answerable for the competence of its opinion. Read as a guarantee, the same report becomes recourse: a promise that if the loan fails, the shortfall can be recovered from the valuer — or rather from the valuer's professional indemnity insurer. The first reading makes the valuation part of the lender's risk management. The second turns the valuer's PI into a free and unpriced credit enhancement.

Nobody writes the second reading down. It lives in behaviour: panels chosen by size of cover rather than quality of judgement; terms demanding unlimited reliance and assignment; fees pushed so low that one trade report spoke of residential reports for as little as £30; and, after every crash, a wave of claims. When house prices fell after 2008, lenders pursued surveyors who had valued at the peak; at least one large independent surveying firm was reported to have been forced out of business by professional negligence claims, and Countrywide took an £11.9m charge in 2010 for an abnormal rise in indemnity claims. Research by the law firm RPC found that as limitation periods ran out, claims against estate agents and surveyors fell from 193 in 2013 to 67 in 2014 — with three large lenders accounting for 86% of claims against property surveyors. The pattern had played out before: the early-1990s crash brought its own rise in lender claims against surveyors.

What the law actually returns

The guarantee reading collides with four legal limits, each of which shifts loss back to the lender.

  • The bracket. A valuation is negligent only if it falls outside the range a reasonably competent valuer could reach — around ±5% for a standard house and ±10–15% or more for unusual property. In 2025 the Court of Appeal stressed that a figure outside the bracket is an indication that something may have gone wrong, not the end of the inquiry. A lender relying on the figure as a fixed point is relying on something the law describes as a range.
  • The measure. Where a claim succeeds, the starting point for damages is the correct value — not the edge of the bracket.
  • The scope of duty. Since SAAMCO (1997), and as restated by the Supreme Court in Manchester Building Society v Grant Thornton (2021), the valuer answers only for the consequences of its information being wrong — broadly, the extent of the overvaluation. The loss caused by a falling market stays with the lender.
  • The lender's own conduct. In Platform Home Loans v Oyston Shipways (1999) the House of Lords held that a lender's imprudent lending can reduce its damages for contributory negligence, and that the reduction is made from the lender's basic loss before the SAAMCO cap is applied. Contractual liability caps then apply after that reduction.

Behind all four sits the insurance itself. RICS sets minimum limits of indemnity scaled to a firm's turnover; for smaller firms they are a fraction of the size of a single commercial loan. And insurers who lose money leave: one trade article reported that losses on the primary layer had run above £500m against premium income of under £40m a year, and RICS itself states that valuation is viable as a service only if valuers can obtain PI at a commercially acceptable cost. A guarantee written by an insurer that can withdraw is not much of a guarantee.

How the law turns a loan loss into a recovery

A lender that has lost money on a secured loan and believes the valuation was wrong does not recover its loss. It recovers what survives a sequence of legal filters, each of which returns part of the loss to the lender. The calculator below runs those filters in order; the table beneath it names each one and the authority for it. Read together, they explain why the guarantee reading of a valuation report fails exactly when lenders reach for it.

First, there must be negligence, and negligence is judged against a bracket, not a point. A figure that a reasonably competent valuer could have reached is not negligent, however far it proves to be from the price eventually achieved. Second, the lender's own conduct counts: contributory negligence reduces damages, and the House of Lords decided in Platform Home Loans v Oyston Shipways that the reduction is applied to the lender's whole loss before the cap, which can wipe out much of a claim. Third, the valuer answers only for what it was responsible for. Under the scope-of-duty principle from SAAMCO, restated by the Supreme Court in Manchester Building Society v Grant Thornton, a valuer who supplied information is liable broadly for the overvaluation, not for a market fall that would have happened anyway. Fourth, contracts and insurance limit what is paid: a reasonable cap in the terms, and the insurer's limit, after which the valuer's own resources — if any — stand behind the claim. Finally, time: claims must be brought within limitation periods that start when the lender first suffers loss, often long before enforcement.

VII.

Focal points, common knowledge and the run

An island of certainty is a focal point that someone took the trouble to build.

Thomas Schelling noticed that people can coordinate without communicating by converging on a focal point — a solution prominent enough that each expects the other to choose it. Asked where to meet a stranger in New York with no arrangement, most of his students chose noon at Grand Central. Michael Chwe went further: to coordinate, a group needs common knowledge — each knows, each knows the others know, and so on — and public rituals exist to manufacture it.

This is what the Borderland's gatekeepers did when they posted "Plan A, Plan B" to the whole community. It is also what a good terms-of-engagement exchange does. The basis of value, the assumptions and the purpose are not merely recorded; they are made mutually known, so that the fragmented work that follows can coordinate on them without further talk. A template issued without conversation records the terms but does not create common knowledge of them. That is why it is so often re-litigated later.

Coordination games have more than one equilibrium. Douglas Diamond and Philip Dybvig, awarded the 2022 Nobel prize with Ben Bernanke, showed that a bank run is one of two equilibria: each depositor's choice depends on what they expect the others to do. A Deadstop in a valuation configuration has the same structure. When the finding lands, every party asks what the others will do — withdraw, re-trade, blame. The careful, verbal, early framing conversation is a device for selecting the good equilibrium before the bad one becomes self-fulfilling.

Tactic

When the news is bad, make it common knowledge in one move, to everyone who must act on it, with the options attached. Leaked news creates many private truths and a run on the relationship. Announced news creates one public truth and a plan.

VIII.

The shadow of the future

Rotation buys independence and spends memory. Repetition buys trust and risks capture.

In a one-shot game, defection often pays. In a repeated game, the shadow of the future can sustain cooperation — the lesson of Axelrod's tournaments and of the theory of reputation. Robert Gibbons and Rebecca Henderson argue that many organisational capabilities rest on relational contracts — informal agreements sustained by the value of future dealings — and that these must solve two problems: credibility (will you keep your word?) and clarity (do we understand the same thing by it?). Credibility might be bought quickly. Clarity takes time.

A valuation panel is a repeated game. Its long shadow is both its strength and its danger. The same valuer, instructed again and again by the same lender, learns the lender's frame — clarity — and has strong reasons to be straight with it — credibility. But the same repetition can teach the valuer what the lender wants to hear. Rotation cuts the shadow short to protect independence; it also throws away the clarity that took years to build. Neither regime is free.

IX.

Designing the game: mechanisms, bases and markets

If you cannot trust the players, change the rules they play by.

Mechanism design, recognised by the 2007 Nobel prize to Hurwicz, Maskin and Myerson, runs game theory backwards: choose the outcome you want, then design rules under which self-interested players produce it. Every lending regime is a mechanism, whether or not its authors call it one.

  • The fixed fee is a mechanism for honesty: a valuer paid the same whatever the figure has no direct reason to inflate it. It provides no reward for effort or depth, which is why it so often buys a template.
  • The Home Valuation Code of Conduct changed the players: it removed loan-production staff from appraiser selection.
  • Mortgage lending value changes the rules. The RICS professional standard Bank lending valuations and mortgage lending value (2nd edition, effective 1 January 2026) describes how Germany prescribes capitalisation-rate floors, building lives and cost allowances, and caps Pfandbrief refinancing at 60% of MLV. Spain requires a downward adjustment where a significant fall within two years is likely, applies a default 10% (15% in volatile markets) where data cannot support an estimate, and requires explicit warnings in the report. Floors and default haircuts are commitment devices: they remove the frame contest before it starts.
  • Basel 3.1 changes the rules again, requiring prudently conservative valuation. In the UK, the PRA dropped its proposed requirement to adjust values towards what would be sustainable over the life of the loan. Implementation is from 1 January 2027. The long view stays in the lender's head — and in the conversation, or nowhere.

Auction and market design, recognised by Nobel prizes in 2012 (Roth and Shapley) and 2020 (Milgrom and Wilson), offers the panel designer one more lesson. Paul Klemperer and Ken Binmore designed the UK's 2000 3G auction, which raised £22.5 billion. Klemperer's conclusion was that what really matters in auction design is attracting entry and preventing collusion. A valuation panel is a market. Its health depends on the same two things: enough independent valuers to keep the game competitive on quality, and rules that stop any pair of players settling into a quiet equilibrium at everyone else's expense.

X.

The renewal game: distress, enforcement and the second life of the valuation

Be viable — then pivot. The receiver is the configuration's renewal strategist.

What "renewal" means here

The word is used in three senses in lending, and the ambiguity matters. In the strategy palette, Renewal is the strategy for a harsh environment: an organisation whose survival is threatened must first restore viability and then pivot to another colour in which it can grow. In loan administration, "renewal" means rolling a facility over at maturity — refinancing or extending it. And in the relationship, renewal is the moment at which lender, borrower and valuer meet again after something has gone wrong and must decide whether to keep playing together.

This section uses the first sense and shows how it governs the other two. A loan enters the renewal environment when distress, covenant breach or default makes the original plan unattainable. From that moment, every choice — to extend, restructure, sell, appoint receivers or sell the debt — is a move in a game among the lender's functions, the borrower, other creditors, tenants, buyers, the solicitors and the valuer. The facility "renewal" of administrative language can be a genuine part of that game or a way of avoiding it: extending a loan to give a viable plan time is renewal; extending it to avoid recognising a loss is extend and pretend.

The two moves, in practice

First, be viable. For a fixed charge or LPA receiver the first phase is stabilisation: secure the building, insure it, collect the rent, deal with health and safety and statutory obligations, stop the bleeding of value through voids, vandalism and lapsing consents. For the lender's business-support or workout function it is the equivalent for the loan: stop new drawings, understand the position, gather the file. The valuer's role in this phase is to tell the configuration what the asset is worth now and on what timetable — usually on several bases, because the right recovery strategy depends on the differences between them.

Then, pivot. The second phase chooses the colour in which value will be recovered. A Classical pivot re-lets and holds or sells an income-producing asset to investors. An Adaptive pivot tests the market in stages, letting the evidence decide. A Visionary pivot repositions or completes a scheme — the riskiest recovery, justified only when a specific, achievable plan unlocks value buyers will pay for. A Shaping pivot brings other creditors, tenants, neighbours or planners into a shared plan, as when a right of way is resolved with the neighbour who holds it.

Renewal, in the palette, has two phases: react early and economise to restore viability, then pivot to another colour to grow again. That is a fair description of a well-run receivership. The first phase secures, stabilises and stops the bleeding. The second chooses the colour in which value will be recovered: re-let and hold (classical), test the market in stages (adaptive), reposition or redevelop (visionary), or bring other creditors, tenants and buyers into a shared plan (shaping).

Distress is also a game among creditors, and the UK once had an unusually good mechanism for it. From the late 1970s the London corporate banking market developed the London Approach: a voluntary, collective way for banks to respond to a company in difficulty, encouraged by the Bank of England. Pen Kent described its purpose in 1993 as helping the financial community preserve value. In game-theory terms it was an institutionalised focal point: a shared expectation of standstill and cooperation that stopped each creditor racing for the exit. Its principles survive in the INSOL statement on multi-creditor workouts.

The dark mirror is extend and pretend: rolling a loan rather than recognising a loss, in the hope that values recover. New York Fed researchers found that in the post-pandemic US market it crowded out new credit, cutting CRE mortgage origination by about 5% since early 2022. Read through the palette, it is a visionary posture adopted in a renewal environment. Read through game theory, it is a war of attrition in which lender and borrower each wait for the other — or the market — to concede.

Who plays the renewal game

The lender's functions change places here (Section III). Business support or the deal team decides between restructuring and enforcement; recovery instructs receivers; risk and finance decide when a loss is recognised; and, in a syndicate, majority lenders decide for everyone. Each has its own reading of the valuation. The solicitors decide what can be sold and how fast: an unresolved right of way, a missing consent or a defective lease is often the cheapest value-add available. The valuer — ideally the original one, who already knows the asset — values options rather than only the asset as it stands. The receiver is the renewal strategist: an agent of the borrower in law, acting for the lender's recovery, and the first person in the configuration to read the whole file.

The valuation's second life

In renewal the original report is read again, by people who did not commission it. The receiver reads it as a recovery map: what did the valuer record about access, condition, consents, letting risk and market depth? Workout and the claims team read it as a possible defendant: was the figure outside the margin of error, was the method negligent, what was the purpose? The two readings pull in opposite directions. A report that recorded the principal risks to realisation gives the receiver a head start and the claims team little to argue about; a report that recorded nothing gives the receiver nothing and the lawyers everything. Cold cases: UK lending cultures as games

In each crisis the direction of deference reversed — and someone was playing a different game from the one on paper.

XII.

Finite and infinite games

Game theory assumes the game exists before the players. The configuration shows the players making the game while they play it.

Game theory has serious critics. Shaun Hargreaves Heap and Yanis Varoufakis argue that its equilibrium analysis borrows a thin, hyper-rational model of the person from neoclassical economics and cannot easily say where preferences, rules or meanings come from. From complexity thinking, Ralph Stacey's complex responsive processes make the same point from the other side: organisations are patterns of conversation in which players, purposes and rules emerge together. A focal point is not found lying in the landscape. It is produced — framed, co-created and connected — by people who did not know in advance that they would need it.

James Carse's distinction helps hold both views. A finite game is played to win; an infinite game is played to continue the play. Each instruction is a finite game, with an end, a winner and rules. Lending is an infinite game made of finite ones. The strategy palette, read this way, is not a classification of environments that exist out there. It is a set of questions a configuration asks itself about the game it is in — and malleability, the palette's second axis, is the admission that the game can be changed by those inside it.

Every instruction is a finite game. Lending is the infinite game they are played inside.

An insurer prices each year's risk and can leave the market. A lender and a valuer are in an infinite game together — or should be. Treating the valuation as a guarantee converts the infinite game into a series of finite claims, each won or lost, and leaves the larger game poorer.

The weaver reappears in what is woven: a lender that weaves recourse into its lending finds recourse, not information, in its files.

Every argument in this paper comes down to one desk, one file and six seats around it. Set up the file and see where the weight falls.

The earlier instruments each isolate one mechanism: the disruption, the palette colour, the game, the gap in knowledge, the legal waterfall. In practice they arrive together. A deal comes in through a broker with an introducer behind it; the asset is a part-let building with a scheme on top; the loan-to-value is ambitious; completion is due in a week; the fee has been squeezed; the seller bought the property four months ago; the report on title will not be ready until after the valuation is signed; and somewhere in credit there is an unspoken assumption that if it goes wrong, the valuer's insurers will pay. None of these facts is unusual on its own. What the simulator is saying

Run a few configurations and three regularities appear. First, the route matters more than most lenders allow. A direct deal puts the lender's own people between the borrower and the valuer. A broker adds energy and a strong pull towards completion. An introducer behind the broker adds a layer the lender often cannot see: how the deal was found, what incentives were offered to whom, whether the purchase was back-to-back. Short ownership by the seller is the UK Finance Mortgage Lenders' Handbook's standing prompt for exactly this reason; in the simulator it lights up the introducer and the solicitors, because those are the seats that hold the facts.

Second, two process facts do most of the work: whether origination talks to the valuer directly, and whether the report on title reaches the valuer before the figure is signed. The first is the main channel for frame pressure, and the reason the Home Valuation Code of Conduct in the United States separated loan production staff from appraisers. The second is the main channel for the information that changes values most. Closing the first and opening the second moves the lender's expected surprise further than any change in fee or timetable.

Third, the guarantee reading rises with exactly the conditions that make the guarantee worthless. High loan-to-value, a squeezed fee, no liability cap and a lender quietly relying on the valuer's cover push the dial towards "guarantee" — and those are the deals on which, as the loss waterfall showed, the margin of error, the scope-of-duty cap and contributory negligence leave most of the loss with the lender. The cure is not a bigger PI limit; it is a lender that keeps its own credit judgement, and a valuer instructed to inform it.

P23 — The desk. Losses that surprise lenders at enforcement will be predicted more strongly by two process facts — whether origination had a direct channel to the valuer and whether the report on title reached the valuer before signature — than by loan-to-value at origination.
